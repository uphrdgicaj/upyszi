# How Young Is Too Young to Take a Statin?

YOU’RE YOUNG, ACTIVE, active, on top of your wellness game—and then your doctor drops the news: your cholesterol is high. And they’re recommending you take meds—statins—to get it down. Ugh.

Why it matters: Elevated cholesterol—especially LDL (a.k.a. the “lousy” kind)—is one of the biggest risk factors for heart attacks, strokes, and cardiovascular disease later on. “Cholesterol levels, even in childhood, are very strong predictors of who could go on to have cardiac events 35 years later,” adds MH advisor John Elefteriades, MD, one of the leading heart surgeons in the United States, citing a study recently published in Circulation. Heart disease is also the leading cause of death for women, claiming more lives each year than all forms of cancer combined.

Cue statins: the common prescription meds that effectively lower cholesterol. But wow. Starting in your 20s and 30s. Is that good? Here’s what to know.

Why Your Doctor Is Recommending Statins Now

THE BIG THING when it comes to taking a statin or not is whether you’re at risk of having a heart-related health event down the line. Generally, statins are used in two situations: If you already have heart disease, or have had a heart attack or stroke. And—this is probably the case if you're in your 20s or 30s—to try to prevent cardiovascular events like heart attacks and strokes, no matter your age, when lifestyle moves alone haven’t been effective in reducing your risk.

The recommendation for a statin isn’t black and white; sometimes it’s based on “very high” LDL cholesterol—a level of 190 mg/dL or higher warrants a statin ASAP. (In general, healthy cholesterol levels for adults mean a total cholesterol level under 200 mg/dL, LDL (“bad”) cholesterol under 100 mg/dL, and HDL (“good”) cholesterol at 60 mg/dL or higher.)

But many times, the decision to prescribe a statin is more nuanced and goes beyond just your LDL number. It also includes family history, genetics, and other factors. For instance, if your dad had a heart attack or stroke before age 55, or your mom did before 65, your doctor might consider a statin even if your cholesterol isn’t sky high (FWIW, “high” LDL levels are considered to be 160 to 189 mg/dL and “borderline high” are 130 to 159 mg/dL).

If your total cholesterol is over 200 when you’re young, docs worry about inherited genetic mutations, which convey a pretty high risk of heart disease. Cardiologist and MH advisor Christopher Kelly, MD, performs genetic testing on these patients and typically prescribes a cholesterol-lowering medication if the mutation is present. Genetic cholesterol abnormalities impact about 1 in 500 people.

Your genetic makeup may also include high levels of a fat called lipoprotein(a). High levels of that can increase your risk of heart disease or stroke. So in determining whether a statin is useful for you, docs will likely check that (which is also known as Lp(a)) as well. The decision around meds may also involve a chest CT scan, which can reveal plaque in the arteries,, says Dr. Kelly.

In these cases, starting a statin at a young age can prevent decades of plaque buildup, says Adedapo Iluyomade, MD, a cardiologist at Miami Cardiac & Vascular Institute, Baptist Health South Florida. “Statins are overall a low-risk medication,” he adds. “I like to think of them as sheep in wolf's clothing.”

Yet, the decision to take statins isn't so much about age as it is about your ultimate risk of getting heart disease and having a heart attack or stroke. “Statins can lower heart attack or stroke risk by 20 to 30 percent,” says Dr. Kelly.

Statin Side Effects: Fact Versus Fear

THERE ARE CURRENTLY eight statins available in the U.S. They’re super safe and very cheap, says C. Noel Bairey Merz, MD, director of the Barbra Streisand Women’s Heart Center at Cedars-Sinai.

[Young Is Too](https://pigmentlab.jcesqc8.com/blog/6548339557.html)

Like all meds, statins do have small risks: small elevations in liver enzymes, a risk of diabetes (usually in those already predisposed), and muscle aches. Certain people may be more likely to experience side effects, too, including if you’re immunosuppressed, if you’re a transplant recipient, or if you’re on multiple drugs. Dr. Bairey Merz notes that studies have also shown a somewhat higher risk of side effects in people of Asian descent, perhaps due to genetic factors.

As for a side effect like rhabdomyolysis, or “rhabdo”—a dangerous breakdown of muscle tissue, which you may have heard linked to statins? While it’s always smart to inform your doctor about any muscle aches that don’t go away, rhabdo is rare with statins, affecting anywhere from one in 100,000 to one in a million people. A study published in The Lancet found that statins were not the cause of muscle pain in more than 90 percent of people with symptoms.

Dr. Bairey Merz says that symptom reporting around statins can sometimes be rooted in something called a “nocebo effect,” which is when you hear about a potential issue, and then experience it. Social media can amplify the problem, she explains.

Today, Dr. Bairey Merz says that pushback against medications can also “play well”—it hits the popular “natural is better” and an “ode to the ‘good old days.’” But she points out that in those “good old days,” dad had a heart attack at 50.

To this extent, Dr. Kelly reminds that while it’s always a good idea to bring up any concerns to your doctor, the small side effects of statins likely receive a lot more attention than they deserve. After all, statins’ benefits are invisible. Says Dr. Kelly: “You don't know about the heart attacks or strokes you didn't have.” Age shouldn’t be the determinant of when to start cholesterol medications; how risky it is for you if you don’t take them is the question to ask.
