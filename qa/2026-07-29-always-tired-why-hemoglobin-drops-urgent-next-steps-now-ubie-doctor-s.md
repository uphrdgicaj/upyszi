Low hemoglobin means your blood carries less oxygen, which often causes persistent fatigue, weakness, and shortness of breath. The most common causes are iron deficiency, vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, chronic disease, blood loss, and, less often, bone marrow disorders.

Optimize your diet with iron- and vitamin-rich foods if appropriate

Feeling constantly tired isn't normal — even if it's common. If you're dragging through the day despite getting enough sleep, your hemoglobin levels may be part of the reason.

Hemoglobin is a protein in your red blood cells that carries oxygen from your lungs to the rest of your body. Oxygen is fuel. When hemoglobin levels drop, your organs and muscles don't get the oxygen they need. The result? Fatigue, weakness, and sometimes more serious symptoms.

Let's break down why hemoglobin levels fall, what that means for your health, and what you can safely do next.

What Are Normal Hemoglobin Levels?

Normal hemoglobin levels vary by age and sex, but general reference ranges are:

Adult men: ~13.8 to 17.2 grams per deciliter (g/dL)

Adult women: ~12.1 to 15.1 g/dL

Pregnant women: Often slightly lower due to increased blood volume

Children: Varies by age

If your hemoglobin levels fall below normal, you may be diagnosed with anemia, a condition affecting millions of people worldwide.

Why Do Hemoglobin Levels Drop?

There are several medically recognized reasons hemoglobin levels decrease. Some are mild and easily treated. Others require more urgent attention.

1. Iron Deficiency (Most Common Cause)

Iron is essential for making hemoglobin. Without enough iron, your body can't produce healthy red blood cells.

Common causes include:

Heavy menstrual periods

Pregnancy

Blood loss (including slow bleeding from the stomach or intestines)

Poor dietary intake

Difficulty absorbing iron (such as in celiac disease or after gastric surgery)

Iron deficiency anemia develops gradually, so symptoms may sneak up on you.

2. Vitamin Deficiencies

Your body also needs:

Vitamin B12

Folate (Vitamin B9)

Low levels of these nutrients can lead to fewer or poorly formed red blood cells, lowering hemoglobin levels.

B12 deficiency is more common in:

Older adults

Vegans or strict vegetarians

People with digestive disorders

3. Chronic Diseases

Certain long-term medical conditions interfere with red blood cell production, including:

Chronic kidney disease

Autoimmune disorders

Cancer

Chronic infections

Inflammatory diseases

In these cases, hemoglobin levels may drop even if iron intake is adequate.

4. Blood Loss

Hemoglobin levels can fall quickly with sudden blood loss or slowly over time with internal bleeding.

Possible sources include:

Stomach ulcers

Colon polyps or colorectal cancer

Frequent use of NSAIDs

Trauma or surgery

Slow internal bleeding can be especially tricky because symptoms may be subtle at first.

5. Bone Marrow Disorders

Your bone marrow produces red blood cells. Conditions that affect marrow function — such as aplastic anemia, leukemia, or other bone marrow diseases — can significantly lower hemoglobin levels.

These causes are less common but more serious.

Symptoms of Low Hemoglobin Levels

Mild anemia may cause few symptoms. As hemoglobin levels drop further, you may notice:

Persistent fatigue

Weakness

Shortness of breath

Dizziness or lightheadedness

If you experience chest pain, severe shortness of breath, or fainting, seek medical care immediately.

How Doctors Diagnose Low Hemoglobin Levels

A simple complete blood count (CBC) measures hemoglobin levels. If your levels are low, your doctor may order additional tests to determine the cause, such as:

Iron studies

Vitamin B12 and folate levels

Kidney function tests

Stool testing for hidden blood

Imaging or endoscopy if bleeding is suspected

The key is identifying the underlying cause — not just correcting the number.

3. Treat the Underlying Cause

Treatment depends entirely on why hemoglobin levels are low.

Possible medical treatments include:

Iron supplements (oral or IV)

Vitamin B12 injections or supplements

Folate supplementation

Medications for underlying disease

Treatment for bleeding sources

Blood transfusion (in severe cases)

Never start iron supplements without testing first. Too much iron can be harmful, especially if your hemoglobin levels are low for reasons unrelated to iron deficiency.

5. Monitor Hemoglobin Levels

After starting treatment, follow-up testing ensures:

Hemoglobin levels are rising appropriately

The underlying cause is being addressed

No new issues have developed

When Low Hemoglobin Levels Are an Emergency

While many cases are manageable, severely low hemoglobin levels can be dangerous.

Seek immediate medical attention if you experience:

Chest pain

Fainting

Severe shortness of breath

Rapid heartbeat at rest

Signs of significant blood loss
