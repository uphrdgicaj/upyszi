What Makes a Good Motorcycle Boot?

The main thing separating a real pair of motorcycle boots from a fashion piece, a work boot, or a casual sneaker is protection. Protection comes in a few different flavors though, so let's do a quick recap on what to look for when choosing a motorcycle boot.

Standardized Safety Ratings

Many of the motorcycle boots you see today come stamped with a standardized safety rating. The most common ratings are CE or EN ratings, and if you can find a boot with either certification, you can rest assured that your feet are reasonably well protected.

These ratings indicate that a boot meets or exceeds European safety standards, which means its abrasion resistance, seam strength, and tear strength have all been independently lab tested and verified. It's important to note, however, that CE and EN ratings only confirm that a motorcycle boot meets minimum safety requirements, so two different boots with the same rating could deliver different levels of protection.

It's also important to point out that not all boots carry these ratings, and that's ok. CE/EN certification is only required for motorcycle gear sold within the European Union, so if a boot you're considering looks safe but doesn't include either rating, here's what to look for when determining its roadworthiness.

Abrasion Resistance

Abrasion resistance is always your first order of business. Any boot that can't protect you from the horrors of road rash isn't a real motorcycle boot, and you shouldn't even consider it. Traditionally, this protection comes from full-grain leather (cowhide, kangaroo, buffalo, etc.) but there are plenty of smart textile options out there nowadays (Cordura, Kevlar, Superfabric, etc.) that protect as well if not better than leather.

Any motorcycle boot worth buying will also include double or even triple-stiched seams to ensure your material of choice stays together during a slide. This type of stitching is a requirement for the aforementioned CE ratings, but if your boot of choice isn't rated, make sure you take a good hard look at the stitching before pulling the trigger.

Lastly, boots with additional abrasion-resistant plates or panels (aka sliders) are always a plus. This is a common feature in road racing boots, and for good reason: By giving the boots a slicker surface to "slide" on, you minimize the likelihood of them "catching" the pavement and sending your body into a tumble. Once you start tumbling you're much more likely to break bones or sustain other injuries, so anything that helps you slide along the ground smoothly is a feature worth considering.

Armor, Padding, and Reinforcement

Abrasion resistance is great, but truth be told you can get plenty of that from any full-grain leather work boot with reinforced seams. What really distinguishes a motorcycle boot as such is the degree of additional protection and reinforcement integrated into the boot.

We consider the bare minimum here to be impact-resistant padding or armor at the ankles. From there we give bonus points to boots with extra protection or reinforcement material in high-wear or accident-prone areas like the shin, toe box, and heel cup.

Ideally, any proper motorcycle boot will take things a step further and add some degree of protection to the sole of the boot as well. This is typically achieved by adding a shank of some kind to the sole, which can range from the fully rigid shanks found in motocross boots to the semi-flexible versions found in riding shoes and touring boots.

Lastly, we'd also like to note that the taller your boot is, the better protected you'll be. Armor at the ankle and below is great, but there's a reason we'd rather go down in a pair of knee-high motocross boots than anything else on the planet. Full-length boots may not be the most fashionable option out there, but if protection is your top priority, they're always your best bet.

Flexion and Torsional Protection

Road rash and broken bones are unpleasant enough, but it's flexion and torsion-type injuries that really deliver the cringe. If you're not familiar with these terms, just imagine your foot turned a full 90 degrees (or more) in any direction you'd rather it not turn, and you'll start to get the picture.

Protection like this is where the best motorcycle boots differentiate themselves from entry and mid-level options. It'll cost you a little extra cash, but stepping up to a boot with these features is always a smart investment. So what does a boot need to keep your ankles pointed in the right direction?

Generally speaking, what you're looking for here is an integrated hinge/brace system at the ankle. Different brands have different names for their proprietary hinge systems, but the purpose of these hinges is to limit the ankle's overall range of motion. The best systems incorporate features that protect against forward flexion and rearward hyperextension, as well as the myriad harmful twisting forces that often accompany crashes.
