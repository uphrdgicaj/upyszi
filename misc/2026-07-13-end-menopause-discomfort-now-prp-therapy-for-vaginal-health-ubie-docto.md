What Is PRP Therapy?

PRP stands for Platelet-Rich Plasma. It's a concentration of your own platelets—tiny blood cells involved in healing—which is prepared from a small blood sample.

Collection: A simple blood draw (similar to routine lab work).

Processing: Spinning that blood sample in a centrifuge separates platelets and growth factors from other blood components.

Injection: The concentrated PRP is injected into the vaginal tissues.

How PRP Addresses Vaginal Dryness

Vaginal dryness in menopause often results from thinning of the vaginal lining (atrophy) due to low estrogen. PRP therapy seeks to:

Enhance tissue regeneration and thickness.

Boost natural lubrication by improving blood supply.

Reduce discomfort, itching, or burning sensations.

Potentially improve sensation and comfort during intercourse.

By harnessing your body's own healing mechanisms, PRP offers a non-hormonal route to restore vaginal health.

The PRP Procedure: Step by Step

Consultation

Review medical history, goals, and alternatives (moisturizers, local estrogen, laser treatments).

Discuss risks, benefits, and costs (PRP is often not covered by insurance).

Blood Draw

A small amount of blood is taken from your arm (10–20 mL).

Centrifugation

The blood is placed in a centrifuge for about 10–15 minutes.

Platelets and growth factors are separated and concentrated into a small volume of plasma.

Injection

Your provider injects the PRP into targeted areas of the vaginal wall and labia.

Local anesthetic or a numbing agent may be used for comfort.

Recovery

Mild cramping or spotting may occur for 24–48 hours.

Avoid sexual activity, tampon use, or strenuous exercise for 2–3 days.

Lubricants or vaginal moisturizers can be used as needed.

Follow-Up

Many women see improvement after one session, but a series of 2–3 treatments spaced 4–6 weeks apart is common.

Maintenance sessions (once or twice a year) may help sustain results.

Benefits Reported by Patients

While individual results vary, many women report:

Reduced vaginal dryness and itchiness.

Improved comfort during daily life and intimacy.

Enhanced tissue elasticity and thickness.

Increased natural lubrication.

A sense of rejuvenation or improved quality of life.

Clinical data is still growing. Early studies and patient surveys suggest encouraging improvements in vaginal health scores and sexual function.

Safety, Side Effects, and Limitations

PRP is generally considered low-risk because it uses your own blood. However, be aware of:

Common, Mild Reactions

Temporary pain, swelling, or bruising at injection sites.

Mild spotting or discharge for 1–2 days.

Less Common Risks

Infection (rare if performed under sterile conditions).

Allergic reaction to numbing agents (if used).

No long-term safety data in large populations yet.

Limitations

Not approved by the FDA specifically for vaginal atrophy (off-label use).

Insurance typically does not cover the cost.

Results can be temporary—maintenance treatments are often needed.

Always choose a qualified provider—ideally a gynecologist or urogynecologist—experienced in PRP injections for vaginal health.

Alternatives and Complementary Options

If PRP isn't right for you, consider:

Vaginal moisturizers and lubricants (water-based or silicone-based).

Low-dose vaginal estrogen creams, rings, or tablets.

Non-hormonal prescription therapies (e.g., ospemifene).

Laser or radiofrequency treatments for vaginal tissue rejuvenation.

Pelvic floor physical therapy for associated discomfort.

You might also try lifestyle measures:

Staying well-hydrated.

Regular gentle pelvic exercises (like Kegels).

Avoiding irritants: scented soaps, douches, or tight synthetic fabrics.

When to Speak to a Doctor

While vaginal dryness often isn't dangerous, certain signs require prompt medical attention:

Severe pelvic pain or heavy bleeding.

Symptoms of infection: fever, foul-smelling discharge.

New or worsening lumps, sores, or ulcerations.

Any concern that something feels "off" or life-threatening.

Always discuss serious or life-threatening concerns with a qualified healthcare professional. If you decide to pursue PRP therapy, ensure you have a thorough evaluation by a trusted doctor who can address any potential risks.

Conclusion

PRP for vaginal dryness is a promising, non-hormonal approach to restoring vaginal tissue health during menopause. By using your body's own growth factors, PRP may help improve lubrication, comfort, and quality of life. Though research is still evolving, many women report meaningful relief with a series of treatments.
