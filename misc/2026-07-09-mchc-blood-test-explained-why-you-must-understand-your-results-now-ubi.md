Explanation

The MCHC blood test is a simple but important part of a routine blood panel. It helps doctors understand how much hemoglobin is packed inside your red blood cells. Hemoglobin is the protein that carries oxygen from your lungs to the rest of your body. Without enough of it—or if it's too concentrated—your body may not get the oxygen it needs to function properly.

If you've recently had blood work done and noticed "MCHC" on your report, this guide will explain what it means, why it matters, and what to do next if your results are outside the normal range.

What Is the MCHC Blood Test?

MCHC stands for Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin Concentration. It measures the average concentration of hemoglobin inside a given volume of red blood cells.

The MCHC blood test is usually included as part of a Complete Blood Count (CBC), which is one of the most common blood tests ordered by doctors.

Unlike some other blood measurements, MCHC doesn't count how many red blood cells you have. Instead, it evaluates how concentrated the hemoglobin is within those cells.

Think of it this way:

Red blood cells = delivery trucks

Hemoglobin = oxygen cargo

MCHC = how full each truck is

If the trucks are underfilled or overfilled, your body may not transport oxygen efficiently.

Why Is the MCHC Blood Test Important?

The MCHC blood test is mainly used to help diagnose and classify anemia, a condition where the body doesn't have enough healthy red blood cells or hemoglobin.

Anemia can cause symptoms such as:

Fatigue

Weakness

Pale skin

Shortness of breath

Dizziness

Cold hands and feet

By looking at MCHC levels alongside other CBC markers—such as MCV (mean corpuscular volume) and hemoglobin—doctors can determine the type and possible cause of anemia.

What Is a Normal MCHC Level?

Normal MCHC values can vary slightly between laboratories, but in adults, the typical range is:

32 to 36 grams per deciliter (g/dL)

If your MCHC falls within this range, it generally means your red blood cells contain a normal concentration of hemoglobin.

What Does Low MCHC Mean?

A low MCHC means your red blood cells have less hemoglobin than normal. This condition is called hypochromia, meaning the cells appear paler under a microscope.

Common Causes of Low MCHC

Iron deficiency anemia (most common cause)

Chronic blood loss (heavy periods, gastrointestinal bleeding)

Thalassemia (a genetic blood disorder)

Chronic inflammatory conditions

When hemoglobin concentration is low, oxygen delivery decreases. Over time, this can lead to noticeable symptoms.

Signs You Might Notice

Ongoing fatigue

Headaches

Brittle nails

Shortness of breath with activity

What Does High MCHC Mean?

A high MCHC is less common but can indicate that red blood cells have an unusually high concentration of hemoglobin.

This can happen in conditions such as:

Hereditary spherocytosis (a genetic disorder affecting red blood cell shape)

Autoimmune hemolytic anemia

Severe burns

Certain laboratory artifacts (testing errors)

In some cases, elevated MCHC may not represent a true medical problem but rather a technical issue with the sample. That's why doctors often repeat testing before drawing conclusions.

Symptoms of high MCHC depend on the underlying cause but may include:

Jaundice (yellowing of skin or eyes)

Enlarged spleen

Dark urine

High MCHC is uncommon and usually requires further evaluation.

How Is the MCHC Blood Test Performed?

The MCHC blood test is done with a standard blood draw:

A healthcare professional draws blood from a vein, typically in your arm.

The sample is sent to a laboratory.

Automated analyzers calculate MCHC based on hemoglobin and hematocrit levels.

No special preparation is usually needed unless your doctor has ordered additional tests.

The procedure is quick and low risk, with minor possible side effects like slight bruising or soreness.

How Doctors Interpret MCHC Results

The MCHC blood test is rarely evaluated alone. Doctors typically consider it alongside:

Hemoglobin

Hematocrit

MCV (Mean Corpuscular Volume)

RDW (Red Cell Distribution Width)

Together, these values help determine:

Whether anemia is present

What type of anemia it may be

Whether further testing is needed

For example:

Low MCHC + Low MCV → Often iron deficiency anemia

Normal MCHC + Low MCV → Possible thalassemia

High MCHC → Possible hereditary spherocytosis

This broader interpretation helps prevent misdiagnosis and ensures proper treatment.

Can MCHC Levels Be Improved?

Treatment depends entirely on the underlying cause.

If Iron Deficiency Is the Cause

Your doctor may recommend:

Iron supplements

Dietary changes (red meat, leafy greens, beans, fortified cereals)

Investigating sources of blood loss

Iron supplements should only be taken under medical supervision. Too much iron can be harmful.

If a Genetic Condition Is Present

Conditions like hereditary spherocytosis may require:

Ongoing monitoring

Folic acid supplementation

In some cases, surgical removal of the spleen

If Chronic Disease Is the Cause

Managing the underlying condition often improves MCHC levels over time.

When Should You Be Concerned?

An abnormal MCHC blood test result is not automatically dangerous. However, it should not be ignored.

Seek prompt medical care if you experience:

Chest pain

Severe shortness of breath

Fainting

Rapid or irregular heartbeat

Signs of significant bleeding

These symptoms could indicate a serious condition and require immediate evaluation.

Even milder symptoms like persistent fatigue or dizziness deserve attention if they don't improve.

Key Takeaways About the MCHC Blood Test

The MCHC blood test measures the concentration of hemoglobin in red blood cells.

It is part of a standard Complete Blood Count (CBC).

Normal range: 32–36 g/dL (may vary slightly by lab).

Low MCHC often suggests iron deficiency anemia.

High MCHC is less common and may indicate specific blood disorders.

Results should always be interpreted alongside other lab values and symptoms.

Final Thoughts

The MCHC blood test provides valuable insight into how well your red blood cells are carrying oxygen. While abnormal results can signal anemia or other blood disorders, many causes are treatable—especially when identified early.

If you're unsure about your results, the best next step is to speak to a doctor. They can interpret your lab work in the context of your overall health and determine whether additional testing or treatment is needed.
