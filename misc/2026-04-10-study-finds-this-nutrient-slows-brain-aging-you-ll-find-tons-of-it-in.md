# Study Finds This Nutrient Slows Brain Aging—You'll Find Tons of It in a Food Already in Your Fridge.

YOU'VE PROBABLY HEARD egg whites are the healthiest way to eat eggs. Yet there is an argument to be made about eating the whole egg. And yes, we're talking about the yolk.

Inside the yellow core of a sunny side up egg lies a treasure trove of nutrients. One of those nutrients in yolk, choline, is most fascinating to brain researchers. In a new study published in the journal Aging and Disease, scientists found lower levels of choline—a nutrient in the B vitamin complex—associated with faster brain aging.

The findings raise a lot of questions about the nutrient’s potential impact on your brain health. It might also help end the slander the health community has about egg yolk.

The Link Between Choline and Brain Aging

For the study, researchers examined several biomarkers from 30 people in their early- to mid-30s. Based on their BMI, 15 people met the criteria for obesity while the other 15 were considered healthy. The researchers noticed less choline circulating in the bodies of people with obesity compared to their non-obese counterparts.

Another observation was more inflammation and higher levels of a blood protein called neurofilament light. When this protein pops up in high numbers, it's usually a sign of damage to brain cells.

This might all sound like two separate issues in obesity. But scientists found there might be some relationship between choline and neurofilament light. Specifically, they observed low choline linked to high levels of the blood protein.

Does this actually translate to an increased risk of brain damage? The study only found a link between choline and neurofilament light. But when the researchers looked at the brains of people who died from mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer’s disease, they noticed a common pattern. The brains of the deceased showed low choline levels.

According to the researchers, the findings suggest low levels of choline may be somehow involved with cognitive diseases like dementia. “Monitoring such markers in early adulthood may be useful for assessing future Alzheimer’s disease risk in individuals prone to obesity,” they wrote in their study.

How Could Choline Help With Brain Health?

You might feel like it's a big stretch to say choline will save your brain. The authors only showed a link between low choline and higher levels of a marker that is then linked to cellular damage that could lead to cognitive decline.

“This is a very small study, and the design of the study does not allow one to determine whether changes in choline are driving any of the other outcomes,” says Matthew Schrag, MD, PhD, associate professor of neurology at Vanderbilt University School of Medicine.

With that said, there is some truth to choline helping your brain stay sharp.

“Choline is an essential nutrient required for brain health and the production of acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter critical for muscle control, memory, and mood,” explains Ramon Velazquez, PhD, assistant professor in the School of Life Sciences and Neurodegenerative Disease Research Center at Arizona State University and study coauthor. “It also helps reduce inflammation and regulate homocysteine levels, which elevations are linked to Alzheimer’s disease risk.”

Low intake of the nutrient has been linked with a higher risk of Alzheimer’s disease before, Velazquez points out. “Additionally, obesity and insulin resistance—both risk factors for Alzheimer’s—are associated with reduced choline levels, suggesting a potential link between choline deficiency, metabolic dysfunction, and neurodegeneration,” he says.

How Much Choline do You Need?

Choline needs vary by age and sex, but the general recommendation is for men to get 550 milligrams of choline a day. (Adult women should strive for 400 to 450 milligrams a day.)

“Most Americans consume only about 50 percent of these levels,” Velazquez says. (He also points out that the NIH guidelines were established in 1998, and there’s some ongoing research to see if they should be raised to better support brain health.)
